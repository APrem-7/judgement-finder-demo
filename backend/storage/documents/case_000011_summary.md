Person G, the petitioner, was detained under the Preventive Detention Act, 1950 (Act IV of 1950). ## Legal Issues Raised  
- Whether the Preventive Detention Act, 1950, as a whole is constitutionally valid.  
- Whether specific provisions of the Act (particularly Sections 12, 14 and the provisions relating to disclosure of grounds for detention) contravene Article 13 (prohibition of retrospective law), Article 19 (freedom of movement), Article 21 (right to life and personal liberty), and Article 22 (procedure for preventive detention).  
- Whether any unconstitutional provision, if found, renders the entire Act void or is severable.  
- The extent of judicial review permissible over the reasonableness of a preventive detention order.  

## Court's Analysis  
The majority examined the purpose of the Preventive Detention Act and held that, except for the provision prohibiting a detainee from knowing the grounds of detention (Section 14), the Act did not infringe the constitutional guarantees of personal liberty and due process.  The Court interpreted “procedure established by law” under Article 21 to include legislative rules that prescribe a fair and transparent process, and found that the Act’s procedural safeguards satisfied this requirement.  Section 14 was declared ultra‑vires because it barred the detainee from receiving the grounds of detention, thereby violating Article 22(5).  However, the Court treated Section 14 as a severable clause, so its invalidity did not affect the remainder of the Act.  A dissenting minority argued that Section 12, which dealt with the duration of detention, was also unconstitutional, but this view was not adopted by the majority.

## Ruling / Holding  
The Court upheld the constitutionality of the Preventive Detention Act, 1950, except for Section 14, which was struck down as ultra vires but held to be severable; consequently, the petitioner’s detention was not declared illegal on that ground.

## Key Legal Principles Established  
- A statute may be partially invalidated without rendering the whole law void if the offending provision is severable.  
- “Procedure established by law” under Article 21 encompasses legislative rules that provide a fair hearing, but does not permit denial of the grounds of detention.  
- Preventive detention is permissible under the Constitution provided that the procedural safeguards of Article 22 are observed.  
- Judicial review of preventive detention orders is limited to assessing compliance with the procedural requirements, not the substantive merits of the detention.
